The chapter, in full

By the end of this lesson you will be able to turn a weak, watery AI answer into a sharp, useful one — using three simple moves.

You open ChatGPT or Grok or Claude. You type a question. You get back a wall of polite text that could have come from a brochure.

That is mush.

We measured this in our own work. Before we fixed our prompts, most answers needed heavy editing. Today the AI produces about 95% of our work product — but only when we give it the right instructions.

The difference is not which AI you use. The difference is what you type in.

A quick word before we start. A prompt is the message you type to the AI. That's it. This whole lesson is about writing better ones.

Why You Get Bland Answers

AI is trained to be helpful. To a machine, "helpful" usually means safe, balanced, and average.

The AI has read millions of web pages, books, and forum posts. It knows what the average answer looks like. So that's what it gives you — unless you force it not to.

We saw this in our own early work sessions. We typed "help me with my marketing." We got back a list of ideas that would fit any business on Earth. None of them fit ours. We wasted hours rewriting.

The fix is not a better AI. The fix is three levers. A lever is a small move that makes a big difference. You control all three, every single time you ask for something.

Lever 1: Give the AI a Job

Tell the AI who it should act as. (Some people call this "role framing." We just call it giving the AI a job.)

Don't say: "Give me marketing ideas."

Say: "You are the head of marketing for a company that has run hundreds of logged work sessions with AI. You have built real systems that put out work every day."

The job changes what counts as a good answer. A helper with no job gives everyone the same advice. A head of marketing with a track record gives you specific moves, in order, that fit the limits you're about to give it.

That is why every one of the seven prompts waiting for you in Chapter 2 begins by giving the AI a job.

Lever 2: The No List

The No list is a short list of things you tell the AI NOT to do. (The formal name is a "refusal clause." Forget that. It's a No list.)

Add lines like these:

  • No advice that could fit any business but mine.
  • No lists without proof behind them.
  • Never use hype words.

Here's why it works. The AI is trained to avoid saying anything that might be wrong. So it hides in the safe middle. The No list blocks the safe middle. Now the only way out is to be specific.

In our own work, this one lever cut fix-up time the most. One of our early prompts had a weak No list, and the AI kept telling us how great we were doing. We added two rules — "Audit from evidence, not self-report" (judge us only on real results, not on how we describe ourselves) and "No inference presented as observation" (no guesses dressed up as facts). The answers went from a pep talk to a real check-up.

Lever 3: The Answer Format

Tell the AI exactly what the answer should look like. (The formal name is "forced output shape." We call it the answer format.)

Don't say: "Tell me what to do."

Say: "Give me exactly these things:"

  • The ripple effects I have not thought of.
  • The one small change that would fix several problems at once.
  • The safe bet — the option with a small downside and a big possible upside.
  • What could prove you wrong.

Named parts force real thinking. Loose paragraphs invite mush. The answer format works like a checklist the AI has to finish.

Every one of the seven prompts in Chapter 2 ends with an answer format plus the prove-it line. That combination is the pattern behind them all.

The Prove-It Line

The prove-it line is the single most powerful thing we ever added to our prompts. It is one line at the end:

"Now tell me what could prove you wrong."

This line turns a confident-sounding answer into an answer you can test. It forces the AI to show what it's assuming. In our own decision records, this line stopped several overconfident plans before anyone acted on them.

We now require this line in every prompt where the stakes are real.

But the title was marketing. There is no secret mode. Those prompts work because of the three levers you just learned. That post became the first lesson in this course — the one you are reading right now. We swapped the hype for the levers.

A Note About New AI Models

AI models change all the time. The levers do not.

The job, the No list, and the answer format work on every major model we have tested. When you save a result, write down which AI model you used. The levers are a method, not a tool.

Do this now

  1. Open Claude (or whichever AI you use). You will see a box at the bottom where you type, like texting.
  2. Pick one real decision you face this week. Write it down in one sentence.
  3. Write a prompt that uses all three levers plus the prove-it line. Use Main Prompt 1 below as your guide, or the Strategist prompt from Chapter 2. Type it into the box and hit send.
  4. Now click "New chat" to start over fresh. Ask the same thing as a plain, one-line question. Compare the two answers.
  5. Make a document in Google Docs or Word. Name it My AI Notebook. Copy both answers into it (highlight the answer, right-click, choose Copy — then right-click in your document and choose Paste). You will add to this notebook in Lesson 3.

You know it worked when the new answer gives you at least one specific next move and at least one thing you could actually check that would prove it wrong. If it doesn't, make your No list stronger and run it again.

Main Prompt 2: Turn Any Question Into a Three-Lever Prompt

1. Gives you a job — who you should act as to answer this well.
2. Adds a No list — three things a lazy answer would do that you must not do.
3. Sets the answer format — the exact pieces the answer must include.
4. Ends with this line: "Now tell me what could prove you wrong."
